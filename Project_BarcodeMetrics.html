01 · Problem

Does DNA follow the taxonomic tree?

The question

Insects and other arthropods are grouped by rank: phylum, class, order, family, genus and species. Within each of these groups, how varied are the DNA barcodes, and how much do two barcodes differ? Does that change as we move down the ranks, from phylum to species?

Background

A DNA barcode is a short stretch of a gene, usually the mitochondrial COI gene, written as a sequence of four nucleotides: adenine, thymine, cytosine and guanine. Specimens of the same species tend to share barcodes, while more distant groups differ more.

AdenineThymineCytosineGuanine

BIOSCAN organizes millions of barcodes by taxonomic rank, from phylum down to species, with each rank split into subgroups. Understanding how much genetic variation sits inside each subgroup explains why some machine learning tasks are harder than others, and needs tools that run at that scale.

DNA barcodes are used to identify species, discover new ones, and study ecology and conservation, faster and more accurately than identification by appearance alone. Barcodes with highly similar sequences are grouped into clusters, each assigned a Barcode Index Number (BIN) and registered in the Barcode of Life Data system (BOLD).

02 · Proposed solution · Method

Two ways to measure DNA

Barcode-Metric describes every subgroup in two ways: how varied its barcodes are, and how far apart they are. Each measure has its own short path, and both run on any barcode dataset, from a laptop to a cluster.

Diversity: the Shannon index

How varied the barcodes are inside one subgroup, for example one species. Each specimen in it, one insect, has its own DNA barcode, and several specimens can share the same barcode. Count how many specimens carry each barcode and turn the counts into shares; rare barcodes add more than common ones, and the contributions are summed. The result is in bits: 0 when all specimens share one barcode, higher when there are more distinct barcodes, more evenly shared.

All 8 specimens share one barcode.

0.00 bits

7 share one barcode, 1 has another.

0.54 bits

Situation 3

Two barcodes, 4 specimens each.

1.00 bits

Situation 4

3.00 bits

  1. 1CountHow many specimens carry each barcode in a subgroup.
  2. 2ComputeThe diversity of that subgroup, in bits:SDI = − ∑ pi log2 pi
  3. 3AverageOver all subgroups of the rank.

Distance: how many letters differ

How many letters must change to turn one barcode into another (the Damerau–Levenshtein distance). It counts four kinds of change:

Change

One letter is replaced by another.

ACGTA→ACGCA

Insert

One letter is added.

ACGTA→ACTGTA

Delete

One letter is removed.

ACGTA→ACTA

Swap

Two neighbouring letters trade places.

ACGTA→AGCTA

Each of these counts as one. The distance is the fewest such steps between two barcodes: zero means they are identical, and a larger number means they are less alike.

  1. 1FilterSkip subgroups with fewer than 4 different barcodes.
  2. 2SampleKeep at most 1,000 barcodes per subgroup.
  3. 3AlignLine the barcodes up letter by letter, with MAFFT.
  4. 4MeasureThe distance between every pair of barcodes.
  5. 5AverageFirst within each subgroup, then over the rank.

Runs with Pandas for smaller datasets and with Apache Spark for millions of barcodes, such as BIOSCAN-5M.

03 · Findings

Diversity across the tree

Run on the 2.5 million unique barcodes of BIOSCAN-5M, both measures fall steadily towards species: groups become less diverse, and their members genetically closer.

BIOSCAN-5M. For each rank: average diversity (Shannon index, in bits) across its groups, and the average number of letters that differ between two barcodes of the same group.

At species level, barcodes differ in only about 17 letters on average, so closely related species become hard to tell apart from DNA alone. Combining DNA with images helps close that gap.

04 · My role

What I built

  1. Processed DNA barcode data from BIOSCAN-1M and BIOSCAN-5M across hierarchical taxonomic ranks.
  2. Computed SDI to quantify genetic richness and evenness within subgroups.
  3. Measured genetic similarity with pairwise Damerau–Levenshtein distances on aligned sequences.
  4. Built dual pipelines in Pandas and Apache Spark, with filtering, sampling and alignment for large-scale analysis.
  5. Summarized and visualized statistics (mean, median, IQR) with distribution plots and box plots.
  6. Produced reusable outputs as Parquet files and DataFrames for downstream bioinformatics and ML.
